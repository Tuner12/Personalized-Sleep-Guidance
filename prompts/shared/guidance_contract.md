# Shared Guidance Contract

Write four connected, reader-facing Sections. Together they should answer: what happened,
what it means, what to do, and how to judge the result. Keep each fact, explanation, action,
condition, and number in the Section where it contributes most.

## Section 1: Summary

Give a selective longitudinal account of the person's sleep. Begin with the usual whole-record
state, then include the change across time or recent comparison that most affects the current
decision. Mention the latest night only when it helps the reader understand whether the recent
pattern continued or changed. Add a dated fluctuation only when it changes the interpretation.
Longitudinal means showing the repeated pattern and its relevant change, not reporting every
available time view.

Usually use three or four short sentences. Select two to four relevant dimensions rather than
reducing the account to sleep duration alone or listing every available metric. Use as many
numeric anchors as the selected comparisons require, but do not repeat a value or comparison
merely to sound complete. Give both time in bed and sleep duration when their difference
changes the interpretation. Name timing, time awake after falling asleep, long wake episodes,
or the share of time asleep when they materially describe the longitudinal pattern. Sleep-stage
and activity details appear only when they affect the case reasoning.

One sentence should normally communicate one comparison or one contrast. When three or more
metrics change in different directions, split the account into shorter sentences. Sentence
order can provide coherence; an explicit transition word is not required between every fact.

State observations, not causes, diagnoses, Evidence interpretation, or advice. Keep the
overall pattern distinct from a recent comparison or unusual night, and never let one night
determine the overall conclusion.

## Section 2: Pattern Analysis

Interpret and prioritize the main repeated pattern. Explain why it matters and include the
main counterpoint or uncertainty only when it changes the conclusion.

Apply selected Evidence to the person's situation rather than narrating cards or studies.
Use a population comparison only when it materially clarifies the decision. State the
person's finding first; treat the comparison as context, not a diagnosis, cause, or exact
personal target. Do not infer unrecorded behavior, exposure, symptom, preference, or sleep
disorder. Pattern Analysis explains the case and does not give instructions.

## Section 3: Personalized Recommendation

Use only the `Advise` and `Assist` functions of the 5As: explain the case-linked advice and
make the next step concrete. The record does not contain the person's goals, willingness,
preferred action, practical constraints, or trade-off priorities, so do not claim shared
agreement or invent those preferences. When an action depends on them, state that dependence
as a condition.

Give a complete set of supported next steps as one to four short, action-titled items. Use
two or three items when distinct selected Evidence supports relevant actions for more than
one sleep dimension; retain one item when it is the complete supported action set. Each item
should contain a clearly personalized reason and a concrete action. Normally write two
connected sentences per item: first identify the observed pattern, change, or strength that
makes the action relevant to this person; then explain what the person can do. Use a value or
comparison from the case when it improves clarity, but do not repeat the full Pattern Analysis. Allowed
item roles are:

- a primary change for an observed and addressable pattern;
- maintenance of a specific dimension that is adequate, stable, or improving;
- one conditional action when the relevant exposure or preference was not recorded; or
- a conditional clinical boundary when persistent concern cannot be resolved from the
  available record.

Begin every item with a bold Markdown title on its own line. Copy that title exactly from
the supporting card's `allowed_action_titles`. Use `canonical_action_title` by default and
choose a listed variant only when it better names the target dimension or condition. Never
invent a title from a number, date, or case-specific phrase. Put personalization in the item
body rather than creating another title variant.

Normally use no more than three direct or maintenance actions, no more than one unobserved
behavioral item, and no more than one conditional clinical-boundary item. A clinical boundary
may follow a specific self-directed action only when selected Evidence supports a persistent
unresolved concern; it must never become a routine closing item. Every item must be permitted
by selected Evidence and address a distinct action family and target dimension. Do not add an
item to fill space, duplicate an action under another title, or combine conflicting actions.
A Reference or Explanation does not create an action by itself, although it may explain or
constrain an action.

Order items by decision priority: address the primary pattern first, then give maintenance
or a secondary action. Follow-up should prioritize the main change and does not need a
separate evaluation for a maintenance item unless maintenance is the primary recommendation.

Prefer a supported action based on observed data over generic sleep-hygiene advice. For an
unobserved factor, make the condition explicit and never state that factor as observed or
causal. The condition may appear before or after the action and may use natural wording such
as `if`, `when`, or `only when`; do not force one sentence template. For maintenance, name
exactly what to preserve. Do not invent a dose, schedule, numeric target, diagnosis,
preference, or promised benefit.

Do not reduce an item to a bare command or a generic sleep-hygiene instruction. The item must
still make sense if read on its own: the reader should understand why it applies to their
record, what to do, and any condition or guardrail. Personalization comes from the observed
sleep pattern, not from inventing lifestyle context.

Prefer concrete subjects and verbs. After stating the relevant sleep finding, name the action
directly. Avoid abstract bridge phrases such as `the best place to start`, `a relevant factor
to change`, `this pattern provides a basis`, `support the target outcome`, or `the routine is
serving the sleep dimension well`. Do not make the reader translate process language into an
action.

Use linking words only when they express a relationship the reader needs. Do not add
`therefore`, a nonessential `which` clause, or `rather than` merely to make adjacent facts
sound connected. Prefer two direct sentences when the relationship is already clear. Never
turn temporal co-occurrence into causation through a transition word. Do not use `while` as
a default bridge between observations. Use it only for one clear contrast; otherwise use
`but`, `and`, or separate sentences. Never use `while` twice in one sentence.
Avoid compressed concessions such as `even as` when they make the reader unpack several
metric directions at once. State the changes in separate sentences or use a simple `but`
when the contrast matters.

Prefer a person-centered verb when it expresses the same fact more naturally. For example,
write `you spent less time awake after falling asleep` instead of `time awake after falling
asleep decreased`, and `you slept longer` instead of `sleep duration increased`. Keep the
same period, direction, comparison, and numeric grounding.

## Section 4: Follow-up

Close the chain in one or two concise sentences. A third short sentence is allowed only when
a conditional factor and a supported clinical next step require separate decisions. Name the main sleep outcome to watch and,
when useful, one personal comparison point. State what a repeated change would mean. When
several actions share an outcome, evaluate them together. Do not create a separate evaluation
for every maintenance or supporting item. A direct recommendation normally needs only the
success signal. Add a no-effect interpretation or next step only when it would change the
reader's decision and selected Evidence supports it. Do not draw a conclusion from one
unusual night.

Write prospectively and directly, for example: `Over the next several nights, see whether
sleep lasts longer than it has recently. If it does, the added time is helping.` For a
conditional factor, a lack of improvement may indicate that the factor is
not an important contributor. If selected clinical-boundary Evidence supports a next step,
state the condition and action clearly in natural prose. For example, either `If the
difficulty continues, speak with a doctor` or `Consider speaking with a doctor when the
difficulty continues` is acceptable. These are examples, not required templates. Avoid
nominalized wording such as saying that a pattern `would be a reason to` take action when a
shorter sentence is clearer. Do not add professional care routinely. Avoid double negatives
and research-protocol wording.

Do not repeat the rationale, list every Personalized Recommendation item again, introduce another behavior, or
add a new recommendation. For a clinical-discussion boundary, state only the condition that
would make the discussion useful. Do not explain both why medical advice is and is not needed,
and do not imply that the discussion itself is a treatment outcome.

## Writing across Sections

Write concise, natural, second-person English. Use readable dates, clock times, durations,
and percentages. Describe the completed record in the past tense. Name the sleep dimension
that changed instead of saying only that sleep became better or worse.
When a general sleep-duration comparison is seven hours, write `7 hours` (or `7 hours or
more`) rather than converting it to `420 minutes`. Use minutes for shorter durations or for
time-awake measures when that is the natural unit.
Keep comparisons grammatical: write `below 7 hours` or `well below 7 hours`, never
`below 7 hours or more`. When less time in bed limits sleep duration, say that directly;
avoid phrases such as `little opportunity for longer sleep`.

Use pronouns naturally when their antecedent is nearby and unambiguous. Repeat the noun only
when `it`, `this`, `that`, `they`, or `these` could refer to more than one metric, period,
condition, action, or outcome. Do not mechanically remove or insert pronouns.

Do not expose metric identifiers, Evidence identifiers, internal roles, statistical
terminology, retrieval language, source technology, or audit vocabulary. Do not discuss the
frameworks or citations in reader-facing text. Remove repetition across Sections before
returning the result.
